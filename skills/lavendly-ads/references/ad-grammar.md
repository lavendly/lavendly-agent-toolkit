# Ad grammar

The rules every ad follows before any category recipe applies: one promise,
a hook and a call to action that make it, timings that hold attention, the
UGC formats, static ads turned into video, variants that teach something,
and the placements' specs. Numbers marked (platform) come from the ad
platforms' own guidance; numbers marked (practice) are working conventions,
not measured on Lavendly.

## 1. The ad card

Write it before the shot list and show it with the director's intent card.

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Product | A 30 ml amber glass serum bottle with a black dropper |
| Audience | Women 25 to 40 with dry skin in winter, scrolling Reels |
| The one promise | Hydration you can see sink in |
| Hook (0 to 3 s) | Macro: one drop lands on skin and spreads |
| Proof (seen, not claimed) | The drop absorbing into the back of a hand, no residue on a fingertip |
| Call to action (last 3 s, held 2 s or more) | "Try it tonight. Link below." over the packshot |
| Placement, aspect, length | Reels, 9:16, 15 s |
| Offer and terms | None, or exactly the user's words |
| Disclosures | None, or the UGC line (section 6) |
| Variant plan | Hook A (the drop) against hook B (the dry-skin close-up); one variable |

The concept gate: retell the ad in three sentences ("It opens on..., then...,
it ends with..."). A muted viewer must get what it is, what it does for
them, and what to do next from the pictures and captions alone. "What it
does" must be seen happening; an answer only the narration gives does not
count. If the gate fails, change the plan, not the prompts.

## 2. One promise, said twice

The hook raises the promise; the call to action cashes it. They use the
same words or the same image.

| Hook | Call to action | Match |
|---|---|---|
| "Cold brew in ninety seconds." | "Ninety seconds. Order yours." | same words |
| A drop spreading on skin | The bottle beside a hand with the same sheen | same image |
| "Your desk cable mess, gone." | "One dock. Every cable. Link below." | same problem |
| Mismatch to avoid: "Sleep better tonight" | "Twenty percent off all pillows" | the offer replaced the promise |

One benefit per ad. A second benefit is a second ad.

## 3. Timings by length

The anatomy (practice): hook 0 to 3 s, context 3 to 8 s, payoff 8 to 18 s,
proof 18 to 25 s, call to action in the last 3 s and held at least 2 s.
Compressed for short lengths:

| Length | Hook | Body | Proof | Call to action | Shots on Lavendly |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 s bumper | 0 to 1.5 s, product in motion | 1.5 to 4 s, one benefit seen | inside the benefit shot | 4 to 6 s, packshot | 2 or 3 |
| 15 s | 0 to 2 s | 2 to 9 s, two or three benefit shots | 9 to 12 s, in use or result | 12 to 15 s | 4 to 6 |
| 30 s | 0 to 3 s | 3 to 10 s problem or desire, 10 to 22 s product as answer | 22 to 27 s | 27 to 30 s | 7 to 10 |
| 60 s | 0 to 5 s | 5 to 20 s world and person, 20 to 45 s product in life | 45 to 55 s payoff | 55 to 60 s | 11 to 15 |

- TikTok (platform): over 63 percent of the highest click-through videos
  show the key message or product in the first 3 s; 9 to 15 s for direct
  response, 21 to 34 s for story ads; tracks above 120 BPM drive higher
  view-through.
- Meta (platform): design for sound off with captions; most ads 15 s or
  less; brand and key visual in the first 3 s.
- YouTube (platform): the ABCD frame, Attract, Brand, Connect, Direct; say
  the brand name in the first 5 s.
- Average shot length (practice): 1.5 to 2.5 s for social ads, 2.5 to 4 s
  for premium and luxury, the packshot held 2 to 3 s. Every Lavendly shot is
  at least one paid clip, so a 15 s ad of 5 or 6 clips is the balance of
  pace and cost; split a clip only when it holds two usable beats.
- Beat maths for a score brief: BPM = 60 x beats / seconds. 6 s at 120 BPM
  is 12 beats, 15 s at 128 BPM is 32 beats (8 bars), 30 s at 128 BPM is 64
  beats, 60 s at 120 BPM is 120 beats. Ask for the tempo in the brief, but
  the music provider may not follow it: never promise exact BPM. The
  delivered track decides where the cuts land; ask the user to confirm the
  downbeats by ear.

## 4. Hook templates

Each is a picture first, a line second. All are honest: the product does
what it does.

| Template | Picture in frame 1 | Line (optional) | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product in motion | the product already moving: a drop falling, a cap twisting off, a shoe landing | none | every category |
| The sound | a close action whose sound is the hook: a click, a pour over ice, a zip | none; the sound carries it | food, tech, fashion |
| The problem | the pain in close-up: tangled cables, a dry patch of skin, a cold coffee | "If your desk looks like this..." | tech, beauty, home |
| The result first | the finished state for half a second, then back to the start | "Wait for the end." | before and after, food, home |
| Direct callout | the product held toward the lens | "Night-shift nurses, this one is for you." | UGC, health |
| The question | a detail that raises a question: a sealed box, a shape in shadow | "What fits in a pocket and..." | launch, tech |
| Pattern break | something enters frame fast and lands: the product drops into a calm still life | none | any feed ad |
| Camera bump | the product nudges the lens, the frame shakes once | "Okay, look at this." | UGC |
| Scale surprise | the product next to something that changes its size reading | none | tech, jewellery |
| Texture macro | a material so close it is abstract, then the focus reveals the product | none | beauty, food, fashion |

Rules: the hook is in frame 1, not after a fade. One gap per hook; two
hooks stacked close neither. The hook shot is the most expensive one to get
right: give it the best model length and the first re-roll if needed.

## 5. Proof and call to action

Proof the viewer can see: the action working (the stain lifting, the
cable clicking in, the drop absorbing), the material up close, the scale in
a hand, the before and after of a real case the user supplied. Never a
star rating, a review count, a percentage or a "dermatologist tested" line
unless the user gives it with its source; never an AI person's testimony.

Call to action: an imperative of 2 to 6 words spoken over the packshot
("Try it tonight.", "Order before Friday.", "Download it free."), captioned,
held at least 2 s with clean negative space for the logo and offer card the
user adds in the app. Numbers spelled out in speech ("twenty percent", "ten
pounds"); captions show the spoken words.

## 6. The five UGC formats

An even split across a batch tests formats, not just hooks. With AI people
each format has limits (see [ugc-style.md](ugc-style.md)): AI people
demonstrate; they never claim to be customers.

| Format | Picture | With AI people on Lavendly |
|---|---|---|
| Entertainment | a skit, a challenge, a small stunt with the product | allowed as an obvious skit; no testimony |
| Street interview | someone asked about the product on a street | only as a labelled skit; never presented as real passers-by; prefer another format |
| Unboxing | hands open the box, lift the product, turn it to the lens | hands only is the safest and most convincing |
| Product review | a person shows features and says what they are | features and use only; a real customer's words only with permission and labelled as a dramatisation |
| ASMR | close, quiet handling: caps, pours, textures, clicks | hands only; the sound is the content |

Disclosure line for any ad with an AI person: "Dramatisation. AI-generated
person, not a real customer." It goes in the post caption (lavendly-publish)
and as an on-screen label the user adds in the app; say both at delivery.

## 7. Static ad to video

The user has a still ad (a photo with a product) and wants it to move. Two
treatments work on Lavendly; ask which one fits, and recommend in one line
(a photographed moment suits A, a designed layout suits B).

**A. Reverse into the still.** The video arrives exactly on the photo.

1. Ask for the photo without its text and graphics (the user removes them
   or supplies the clean original). `import_asset` it.
2. One scene whose first frame is the clean photo. Motion prompt: a camera
   move away from the framing plus motion that reads the same played
   backward (a light shimmer, a slow sway of fabric or leaves). Never steam,
   pours, falling things or walking: reversed, they read wrong.
3. `edit_clip` with `reverse: true`: the clip now ends on the photo, pixel
   for pixel at its last frame. Native audio `off` (reversed sound reads
   wrong); the score carries it.
4. The hold: a second short scene opening on the same photo with "locked-off
   camera, nothing moves but a faint light shimmer", trimmed to 2 to 3 s.
   The user lays the original text and logo over it in the app.

**B. The hand builds it.** A designed layout assembles itself by hand.

1. Ask for a clean background plate (the set without the product) and the
   packshot. `import_asset` both.
2. Scene 1 opens on the empty plate. Motion: one real hand places the
   product in one beat, with a contact shadow, then leaves frame. "A real
   human hand, one smooth placement, the product lands and stays still."
   SFX only (the set-down tap).
3. Scene 2 opens on the packshot frame, locked-off, held 2 to 3 s for the
   user's text. Hard cut on the hand leaving frame.

Last-frame conditioning is not reachable through Lavendly, so the end of a
generated move is never pinned; the reverse trick and the hard cut to a
real frame are how the ad lands on the real picture.

## 8. Variants

- Change one variable per variant: the hook shot, the first line, the music
  brief, the call to action, the length. Two changes teach nothing.
- Variants that cost no clip: narration line (voice about 1 credit), score
  (2 or 3 credits), caption style, shot order (`move_clip`), trims, the end
  hold. Render A, change one thing with `update_track` or a new
  `attach_track`, render B. Both renders stay in `list_renders`.
- A new hook shot costs one clip. Render A first. Then, with the user's yes,
  replace the hook scene in the same workflow with `update_workflow` (the
  old hook clip leaves the canvas; its render stays in `list_renders`),
  generate only the new hook, `move_clip` it to the start, render B.
- Offer two or three variants, not a batch of twenty. Label each render with
  the one thing that changed.

## 9. Placements, specs and safe zones

| Placement | Shape | Export (Creator and up) | Keep clear at 1080x1920 (practice) |
|---|---|---|---|
| TikTok | 9:16 | 1080x1920 | top 150 px, bottom 320, left 60, right 120 |
| Reels | 9:16 | 1080x1920 | top 210, bottom 340, left 40, right 100; Meta: top 14 percent, bottom 35 percent, sides 6 percent |
| Shorts | 9:16 | 1080x1920 | top 150, bottom 350, left 60, right 150 |
| Universal vertical | 9:16 | 1080x1920 | top 210, bottom 450, left 60, right 150 |
| Meta feed | 1:1 or 3:4 | 1080x1080 or 1080x1440 | product inside the centre square |
| YouTube | 16:9 | 1920x1080 | lower fifth free for the skip button and captions |
| LinkedIn | 1:1 or 16:9 | 1080x1080 or 1920x1080 | captions on, sound off |

- Free and Starter plans export 720p (720x1280, 1280x720, 1080x1080 square,
  810x1080 for 3:4); Creator and up 1080p. 180 s per film at most.
- Vertical framing: the product and faces in the band from 15 to 55 percent
  of frame height, above the caption band. Captions burn bottom-aligned by
  default.
- One film, one shape: the export takes the first clip's aspect; a second
  placement in another shape is a second workflow (the prompts carry over;
  the clips are paid again).
- Key content inside the 1:1 centre square survives any later crop.

## 10. The cover-the-logo test

Swap the logo and colours in your head. If the same ad would sell a
competitor's product just as well, the hook is generic. Find the one thing
only this product does or looks like, and put that in frame 1.

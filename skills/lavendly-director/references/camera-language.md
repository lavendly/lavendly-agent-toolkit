# Camera language, composition and movement

Choose framing and camera behaviour for a story beat, then write it in plain
words the model can follow. Effects are tendencies, not guarantees. A still
camera with a moving subject is the most reliable shot a generative model
makes; add movement only when it adds information or feeling.

## 1. Shot sizes

| Size | Use it for | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| Extreme wide, establishing | Geography, scale, isolation, subject against environment | Keep the subject readable if it acts |
| Wide, full | Whole subject, body movement, a complete object, spatial relations | |
| Medium wide | Hands and body language with context | |
| Medium | Demonstrations, presenters, dialogue, expression plus gesture | |
| Medium close-up | Face and upper body with a little context | |
| Close-up | A reaction, a product feature, a decision, a meaningful object | |
| Extreme close-up, macro | Texture, a small action, surface quality | The viewer must still know what they see |
| Insert | One action or detail: a switch pressed, a knife cut, a label, a key turned | The contact point in frame |
| Over the shoulder | Two people, or a viewpoint on a task | |
| POV | The viewer inside a character | Plausible movement and visible body parts |
| Two-shot | Relationship and blocking | Faces and actions not hidden |

Across a film, change size between neighbours. Two equal sizes in a row read
as a slideshow.

## 2. Height and angle

- Eye level: neutral, direct, trustworthy. Explanation, natural dialogue.
- Low angle: imposing, monumental. Not a default "hero" trick; mind distortion.
- High angle: layout, process, a subject made small. Not automatically weak.
- Overhead, top down: food, craft, packing, diagrams, tabletop processes.
  Check shadows, hand orientation, legibility.
- Ground level: footsteps, wheels, floor-level motion, tactile scale.
- Dutch tilt: instability. Rare and motivated.
- Three-quarter: reveals volume and form; the default for products and faces.
- Profile: silhouette, direction of travel, an observed private moment.
- Back view, silhouette: mystery or viewpoint; keep enough context to read.

## 3. Composition

- Rule of thirds: a starting point, not a law.
- Centred symmetry: order, precision, impact. The geometry must truly align.
- Negative space: breathing room, isolation, space reserved for a title.
- Leading lines: guide the eye to the subject or through the frame.
- Foreground framing: depth, looking into a space. Never hide the subject.
- Layered depth: foreground, subject, background separated by distance,
  light, focus or motion.
- Headroom and look room: space above the head and in the direction of the
  look or the movement.
- Frame in a frame: doors, windows, arches, machinery.
- Clean background: when the message must be read at once.
- Reflections: elegance when they make sense; name what they reflect.

## 4. Lens perspective and depth of field

Describe the look, not lens metadata the model cannot obey.

- Wide perspective: environment and distance exaggerated; close objects grow.
- Normal perspective: familiar, balanced; observational scenes, natural
  product views.
- Telephoto perspective: compressed distance, the subject isolated.
- Macro: fine texture, very shallow focus.
- Deep focus: several layers readable; demonstrations, architecture.
- Shallow focus: one plane; never blur context the viewer needs.
- Rack focus: attention moved between two subjects, with a reason and a
  clear start and end. Ask for it only when the shot has nothing else to do.

## 5. Movement vocabulary, one move per shot

| Say this | It does | Not this |
|---|---|---|
| locked-off camera, no movement | clarity, tension, product detail, before and after | "no shaky camera" |
| slow push-in from medium to close on <subject> | intimacy, discovery | "cinematic zoom" |
| slow pull-back revealing <context> | scale, distance, a reveal | "epic reveal" |
| slow pan left to right following <subject> | follow or reveal, never aimless | "sweeping pan" |
| tilt up from <base> to <top> | height, vertical action | |
| lateral tracking at walking pace, parallax on <foreground> | layers, controlled depth | "smooth cinematic tracking" |
| the camera glides alongside <subject> at their pace | travel with a subject; keep speed and distance constant | "dynamic follow" |
| slow orbit, a quarter turn around <object> | form, inspection; keep the background plausible | "360 orbit" |
| crane rising from ground level until <subject> is small | vertical space, perspective change | "drone shot" |
| handheld, subtle motion, following from behind | immediacy, presence; controlled, motivated | "shaky cam energy" |
| static camera, the subject moves through the frame | the most believable shot a model makes | |

Zoom changes the lens, dolly moves the camera; say which. Parallax is a
consequence of a lateral move past layers, not an effect to ask for.

## 6. Blocking and screen direction

- Say where subjects and objects are in relation to each other.
- Keep movement direction constant across adjacent shots unless a crossing
  is shown.
- Keep the 180 degree axis in dialogue and action unless the crossing has a
  visible cue.
- Match the movement at a cut on action.
- Entrances, exits, handoffs and object interactions must be physically
  possible in the frame described.
- Object positions, hand dominance, wardrobe and background layout do not
  change between shots without a reason.

## 7. Choose with intent

Before writing a shot, answer:

1. What should the viewer notice first?
2. What does the framing reveal or hide?
3. What feeling or relationship does it support?
4. Why is movement needed, if any?
5. What must stay readable?
6. How does it connect to the shot before and after?

No answer: simplify the shot.

# Visual metaphors: explainer

Concrete, filmable pictures for abstract ideas. A video model draws objects,
materials, light and motion well; it draws text, numbers and diagrams
badly. So every idea here becomes an object that changes.

## How to use a metaphor

1. Declare it once in the narration ("think of interest as a snowball").
   The viewer must know the picture is a comparison.
2. Keep one metaphor per section. Two metaphors for one idea confuse; a new
   metaphor needs a new heading in the plan.
3. Pick a metaphor with an event in it (something fills, tips, grows,
   breaks, connects). A still object reads as a slideshow.
4. Show direction, never a value. "The left jar holds more" is fine; "the
   jar shows 38 percent" is not. The number is spoken.
5. Write the destination and the count: "exactly three marbles roll into
   the left cup and stop", never "marbles roll around".
6. Avoid the tired ones unless twisted: a lightbulb for an idea, a
   handshake for trust, chess for strategy, gears for a system, a rocket for
   growth, a maze for confusion. Viewers stop seeing them.
7. Keep the look: the metaphor uses the film's style line and light.

Columns: the idea, the picture (with the change it shows), the camera that
reads it best, what to watch for.

## 11. Choosing between literal and metaphor

- Literal first when the thing itself can be filmed (a plant, a knot, a
  machine, an animal). A metaphor is for what has no body: money, time,
  ideas, systems, probability.
- A metaphor never carries a claim the narration does not make.
- For news, history and health, prefer literal objects and places to
  metaphors that judge (no ticking bomb for a policy, no wilting flower for
  a group of people).
